Logistic Regression: Intro & Interpreting Coefficients

This sheet is about modeling the probability of a yes/no outcome. Example: weak grip strength in 4,184 Korean men aged 45 or older (KLOSA study).
From linear regression to logistic regression
The main idea

Linear regression models a continuous outcome, such as grip strength in kg. When the outcome has two categories, we call the outcome binary / continuous / normal, and we model it with logistic regression.

A logistic model does not predict the outcome value itself. It predicts the probability / variance / residual of the outcome.

Outcomes such as having a disease or not, being cured or not, and dying or not are very common / rare / unethical in health research. This is why logistic regression is widely used in public health.

Our example uses KLOSA men. They are labeled as having weak grip (coded 1) when grip strength is at or below 27 / 45 / 60 kg.

Why a straight line does not work for probabilities
The gold dots show the proportion with weak grip in 5-year age groups. The proportion is 3% for ages 45 to 49 and 79% for ages 85 and older.

A linear model does not work well for probabilities. It predicts probabilities below 0 / 0.5 / -1 for the youngest men. For older ages, it would eventually predict probabilities above 1 / 100 / 0.5. It also does not match the shape of the data. The solution is an S-shaped curve called a sigmoid / parabolic / exponential curve.

How to fit and use the model
Fit the model in R, then read summary(mod)
mod <- glm(weak_grip ~ age, family = binomial, data = klosa) summary(mod) Estimate Std. Error z value Pr(>|z|) (Intercept) -8.8243 0.3193 -27.64 <2e-16 *** age 0.1168 0.0048 24.57 <2e-16 ***

Logistic regression is fit with the glm / lm / aov function. glm stands for generalized / gaussian / graphical linear model. Because the outcome has two categories, we must set family = binomial / gaussian / poisson.

The age estimate is positive. This means the probability of weak grip rises / falls / stays flat with age. The p-value is very small. This shows the effect is significant / negligible / causal.

The logistic curve predicts probabilities
\[p = \dfrac{1}{1 + e^{-(\beta_0 + \beta_1 x)}}\]
\[p = \dfrac{1}{1 + e^{-(-8.82 + 0.1168 \cdot \mathrm{age})}}\]

By design, this function only outputs values between 0 and 1. Every prediction is a valid probability / odds ratio / residual.

When you enter an age, you get a point on the S curve.

In R, you can compute these with the predict / summary / anova function. You do not need to substitute by hand.

Odds and log odds: what the slope actually changes
A straight line on the log odds scale
\[\ln\!\left(\dfrac{p}{1 - p}\right) = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x\]

The quantity \(p / (1 - p)\) is called the odds / risk / rate. Its natural log is called the log odds.

On the probability scale, the change per extra year is not fixed / always 0.1168 / always 12%. The S curve is gentle at the ends and steepest in the middle / tails / intercept.

What is fixed is the change in log odds: every extra year of age adds 0.1168 / 0.63 / 8.82 to the log odds, at every age. When you plot the model against log odds, the fitted model is a perfectly straight / S-shaped / vertical line.

Odds ratios: what you report in a paper
How to convert a coefficient to an odds ratio
# broom: exponentiate the estimates tidy(mod, exponentiate = TRUE, conf.int = TRUE) term estimate conf.low conf.high p.value age 1.12 1.11 1.13 <0.001

Log odds are hard for readers to understand. Papers usually report the odds ratio / log odds / z value instead. In R, broom's tidy() with exponentiate = TRUE / conf.int = TRUE / family = binomial reports odds ratios for you. You can also exponentiate the coefficient by hand:

\(\mathrm{OR} = e^{\beta_1} = e^{0.1168} \approx\) 1.12 / 0.89 / 2.72

In words: "Each additional year of age was associated with 12 / 1.12 / 0.12% higher odds of weak grip (OR 1.12; 95% CI 1.11 to 1.13; p < 0.001)."

Categorical predictors
mod3 <- glm(weak_grip ~ age_grp, family = binomial, data = klosa) term estimate # exponentiated age_grp65-74 5.22 age_grp75+ 14.44

With a categorical predictor, R treats the first / last / largest level (here, ages 45 to 64) as the reference / outcome / intercept group. Each odds ratio compares one group with the reference group.

"Compared with men aged 45 to 64, men aged 65 to 74 had 5.22 / 14.44 / 1.12 times the odds of weak grip. Men aged 75 and older had 14.44 / 5.22 / 0.522 times the odds."

How to describe an odds ratio in words

This table shows common odds ratios and how to describe them in words.

OR 1.12The odds are 12 / 112 / 1.2% higher
OR 1.50The odds are 50% higher
OR 2.00The odds are twice / half / 20% of as high
OR 4.00The odds are four times as high
OR 0.80The odds are 20% lower / higher / equal
OR 0.50The odds are half / twice / 5% of as high
An OR of exactly 1 / 0 / 100 means the predictor is not associated with any change in the odds.
Correct or misconception?
"Each extra year of age multiplies the odds of weak grip by about 1.12."
This is correct. A fixed addition on the log odds scale is a fixed multiplication of the odds. Each year multiplies the odds by e^0.1168 = 1.12.
"An OR of 1.12 per year means the probability of weak grip rises by 12 percentage points each year."
This is a misconception. The odds rise by 12% per year, not the probability. The change in probability varies along the S curve, so you cannot state a fixed change in percentage points.
"For very old ages, the fitted logistic curve will eventually predict a probability above 1."
This is a misconception. The logistic function has a maximum of 1. Its predictions approach 1 but can never exceed it, at any age.
