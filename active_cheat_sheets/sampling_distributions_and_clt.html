Sampling Distributions & the Central Limit Theorem

Sampling variability and the emergence of the normal distribution
Sampling variation
Different samples, different statistics

A sample statistic is one estimate / parameter / census of an unknown population parameter. Even with perfect randomization / rounding, two random samples from the same population will tend to produce different statistics.

That sample-to-sample wiggle is sampling variation / bias / measurement error.

Means hop around the target
Each navy dot is one sample mean; they scatter around the true mean.

In the SBP example, the true mean in the 200,000-person dataset is 119.1 mmHg, but samples of 50 gave means like 120.9, 117.5, and 121.4. Sample means tend to cluster / spread / jump near the population mean / sample mean / median, but they vary / match / repeat from sample to sample.

The sampling distribution
Data distribution vs sampling distribution

A data distribution plots the raw values / sample means / errors from one sample, such as one histogram of individual blood pressures. A sampling distribution plots a statistic / person / variable across many possible samples.

Definition: the sampling distribution of a statistic is its distribution over every possible random / biased / fixed sample of a given size / mean / shape from the population. We usually approximate it by simulation or theory / guessing / rounding.

Many sample means build a curve
Histogram of many repeated sample means; the bars pile up in a bell shape centered on the dashed true-mean line.
Larger n means less wiggle

When n increases from 50 to 200 to 400 to 800, the sampling distribution becomes narrower / wider / shifted, while staying centered / skewed / lower on the true population mean. Larger samples mainly buy precision / bias / a new mean.

This is why "more people per sample" reduces sampling variation of the sample mean.
Same center, shrinking spread
As sample size increases, the variation of the sample mean (standard error) gets smaller.
The Central Limit Theorem
What the CLT says

The CLT describes what happens when you repeatedly / once / never draw random samples and track a sample statistic such as the sample mean:

  • What it says: For sufficiently large n, the sampling distribution of the sample mean is approximately normal / uniform / right-skewed, even when the parent population is non-normal.
  • What it is not: A large sample does not mean that the data distribution of the raw data will be normal. The CLT is about the distribution of the sample statistic / raw data, that is, the sampling distribution / data distribution / population.
Ticket-bowl example

Imagine tickets numbered 0 to 100, with each number appearing once. The parent distribution is uniform / normal / skewed, because low, middle, and high ticket numbers are equally available. But if we repeatedly draw 10 tickets and average them, those sample means / raw tickets / outliers pile up near 50.

The individual tickets are flat, but the repeated averages form a symmetric bell around 50.
One payoff: one sample gives a margin of error

Since the sampling distribution is normal and centered on μ, about 95% of sample means land within approximately 2 / 1 / 3 standard errors of the true mean. Turned around, your one sample mean is very likely within 2 SE of μ, so you can report a 95% interval estimate of the form x ± 2·SE.

Worked: a sample of n = 100 gives x = 119 mmHg with sample SD s = 15. Then SE = 15 / √100 = 15 / 0.15 / 1.5, so a rough 95% interval is 119 ± 3 / 30 / 1.5 mmHg.

x ± 2SE = 119 ± 3

We never measured the whole population, yet the CLT lets us remove / ignore / quantify our uncertainty rather than just hoping the sample mean is close.
