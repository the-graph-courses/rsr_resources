Odds, log-odds, and odds ratios

Probability, odds, and log-odds

In logistic regression we move between three ways to describe how likely an event is: probability, odds, and log-odds. They all describe the same chance, but they use different number scales. In addition, changes in log-odds can be reported as odds ratios, and this is what you typically report from logistic regression in R. This practical gets you some practice with all these terms.

We begin with probability. Imagine 10 people. An event happened to some of them, for example they passed a test. The coloured dots are the ones it happened to.

Probability asks: out of everyone, what share did it happen to? Here it happened to 4 of 10 people.

$p = \dfrac{4}{10} = 0.40$

Probability is always between 0 (never) and 1 (always).

Here is another group of 10 people. What is the probability that the event happened to a person?

Count the coloured dots, then divide by 10. Write it as a decimal, for example 0.5.

Odds compare the two sides

Probability compares the event to the whole group. Odds compare the two sides directly: how often it happens (or happened), against how often it does not.

Here are 10 people. The event happened to 8 and did not happen to 2.

$\text{odds} = \dfrac{\text{times it happens}}{\text{times it does not}} = \dfrac{8}{2} = 4$

Odds of 4 means the event happens 4 times as often as it does not. You can also say it is 4 times as likely to happen as not.

Odds below 1 and odds of 1

Now take another group of 10 people. The event happened to only 2 and did not happen to 8.

$\text{odds} = \dfrac{2}{8} = 0.25$

The odds are below 1. The event is less common, or less likely, than not.

When the two sides are equal, for example 5 people and 5 people, the odds are exactly 1. That is a 50-50 chance.

Odds above 1: the event is more common, or more likely, than not.
Odds equal to 1: a 50-50 chance.
Odds below 1: the event is less common, or less likely, than not.

The odds of an event are 0.25. Is the event more or less common (or likely) than not?

Odds below 1 mean the "happens" side is smaller than the "does not" side, in counts or in probability.

The event happened to 6 people and did not happen to 3. What are the odds?

Odds = times it happens ÷ times it does not = 6 ÷ 3.

Probability and odds

Probability and odds describe the same chance. With probabilities, odds is how likely the event is, divided by how likely it is not:

$\text{odds} = \dfrac{p}{\,1-p\,}$

What is a log?

A log counts how many times you multiply to reach a number. On this ruler the counting begins at 1, and each step to the right multiplies by 10. The log of a number is the number of steps needed to reach it.

The number 1 needs no steps, so its log is 0. The number 10 needs one step, so its log is 1. The number 100 needs two steps, so its log is 2. The number 0.1 is one step backwards, so its log is minus 1.

Log-odds uses steps that multiply by a fixed mathematical constant called e, which is approximately 2.718, instead of by 10. The rules below hold for every step size, and R computes these logs for you.

A number above 1 has a positive log.
The number 1 has a log of 0.
A number below 1 has a negative log.

Is the log of 0.3 (a number below 1) positive or negative?

Below 1 means you had to divide (shrink) to get there. That counts as steps backward.

Log-odds

Take the odds, then take the log. The result is the log-odds, also called the logit.

$\text{log-odds} = \ln(\text{odds}) = \ln\!\left(\dfrac{p}{1-p}\right)$

The diagram below links the two scales. The left line is probability, from 0 to 1. The right line is log-odds. Each dotted line links one probability to its log-odds, and it passes through the odds on the way.

Probabilities crowd near 0 and 1 on the left, but their log-odds spread out evenly on the right.

The point p = 0.50 has odds 1 and log-odds 0, in the middle.
Very likely: the log-odds is a large positive number.
Very unlikely: the log-odds is a large negative number.

On the diagram, which probability has log-odds equal to 0?

Log-odds 0 is the middle of the right line. That matches a 50-50 probability.

At p = 0.25 (below the middle), is the log-odds positive or negative?

p = 0.25 is below 0.50. Below 0.50 the odds are below 1, and the log of a number below 1 is negative.

Guess the log-odds from a picture

You can estimate the log-odds just by looking. Each square is one person. A coloured square means the event happened to that person.

Half coloured (5 of 10): log-odds is about 0.
More than half: log-odds is positive.
Less than half: log-odds is negative.
The further from half, the larger the number.

The event happened to only 3 of 10 people. About what is the log-odds?

3 of 10 is less than half, so the log-odds is negative.

Why log-odds matter for logistic regression

Probability stays between 0 and 1. A straight line cannot fit well on that scale.

Log-odds can be any number. It is balanced around 0. Logistic regression uses a straight line on the log-odds scale. You saw this fitted line for weak grip and age in the lessons:

$\text{log-odds of weak grip} = -8.82 + 0.117 \times \text{age}$

On the probability scale, the same fitted model is the S-shaped curve you saw in the lessons:

The fitted probability of weak grip by age, from the KLoSA model.

Each extra year of age adds 0.117 to the log-odds of weak grip. Later in this practical we turn that slope into an odds ratio.

Logistic regression fits a straight line on which scale?

A straight line works on log-odds because that scale has no limit at 0 and no limit at 1.

Here is a real example. In a drug trial, 1 patient was cured and 9 were not cured. What are the odds of being cured?

Odds = number cured ÷ number not cured = 1 ÷ 9 ≈ 0.11.

Those odds of about 0.11 are below 1. So the log-odds will be:

Odds below 1 sit below the 50-50 point. The log of a number below 1 is negative.

The odds ratio compares two groups

So far we looked at one group at a time. Most research questions compare two groups. For example: does a drug change the odds of being cured?

drug group
odds = 8 ÷ 2 = 4
no-drug group
odds = 5 ÷ 5 = 1

To compare the two groups, divide one odds by the other. The result is the odds ratio, written OR.

$\text{OR} = \dfrac{\text{odds in drug group}}{\text{odds in no-drug group}} = \dfrac{4}{1} = 4$

The drug group has 4 times the odds of being cured.

Here are two new groups of 10 people. The odds of the event are shown under each group.

group A
odds = 6 ÷ 4 = 1.5
group B
odds = 2 ÷ 8 = 0.25

What is the odds ratio of group A compared with group B?

OR = odds in group A ÷ odds in group B = 1.5 ÷ 0.25.

Reading an odds ratio

OR above 1: the first group has higher odds.
OR equal to 1: the two groups have the same odds. No difference.
OR below 1: the first group has lower odds.

The order matters. The OR of A compared with B is the odds in A divided by the odds in B. Always check which group is on top and which group is the comparison.

A study compares two groups and reports an odds ratio of 1. What does that mean?

An OR of 1 means the odds on top and the odds below are equal. Equal odds means no difference between the groups.

Odds ratios in words

Papers rarely print the phrase "odds ratio of 1.5". They turn it into words. Here is the usual wording.

Above 1: percent higher is the OR minus 1. Below 1: percent lower is 1 minus the OR.

A study reports an odds ratio of 0.8 for exercise and weak grip. Which sentence is correct?

Below 1, the percent lower is 1 minus the OR. Here 1 minus 0.8 = 0.2, so 20% lower odds.

From log-odds to odds ratio

Back to the fitted line from the lessons:

$\text{log-odds of weak grip} = -8.82 + 0.117 \times \text{age}$

Each extra year of age adds 0.117 to the log-odds. Remember the log ruler: adding on the log scale is multiplying on the original scale.

To undo the log, raise e to that power. This is called exponentiating.

$e^{0.117} = 1.124$
Adding 0.117 to the log-odds is the same as multiplying the odds by 1.124. So each extra year of age gives 1.124 times the odds: an odds ratio of 1.124, about 12% higher odds per year.

The same percentage at every age

The odds ratio has a very useful property. For every one-year increase in age, the odds of weak grip rise by about 12%, and this holds at every age. To make the jumps easier to see, the diagram below uses ten-year steps. Over ten years the odds are multiplied by 1.124 ten times, which comes to about 3.2.

The fitted probability curve, with the odds at ages 50 to 90 shown underneath.

This can seem surprising. The probability curve flattens as it climbs: between ages 80 and 90 the probability rises by less than it did between ages 70 and 80. How can the odds keep growing by the same factor?

Probability has a ceiling at 1, so its jumps must shrink near the top. Odds have no ceiling. As the probability presses closer to 1, the odds keep multiplying by the same factor, about 3.2 per decade. This is why one odds ratio can describe the whole curve.

Between ages 80 and 90, the probability of weak grip rises by less than it did between ages 70 and 80. What do the odds do over those same ten years?

Only the probability jumps shrink near the top, because probability cannot pass 1. The odds have no ceiling, so each ten-year step multiplies them by the same factor.

Read from the diagram: the odds of weak grip at age 70 are about 0.52. Ten more years multiply the odds by about 3.2. What are the odds at age 80?

Multiply the two numbers: 0.52 × 3.2.

You exponentiate a logistic regression coefficient (you raise e to the power of the coefficient). What do you get?

The coefficient is a difference in log-odds. Exponentiating turns that difference into a ratio of odds.

The output in R

Here is the model from the lessons, followed by tidy() from the broom package. This is the same output you saw in the coefficients video.

klosa <- read_csv("klosa_men_grip_45plus.csv")
mod <- glm(weak_grip ~ age, family = binomial, data = klosa)
broom::tidy(mod)
term        estimate std.error statistic p.value
(Intercept)  -8.82    0.319       -27.6   <2e-16
age          0.117    0.00475      24.6   <2e-16

The estimate column is on the log-odds scale. The value 0.117 is the slope you already know.

In the output above, what does the estimate of 0.117 for age mean?

The model fits a straight line on the log-odds scale, so the slope is a change in log-odds, not in probability.

exponentiate = TRUE

You do not exponentiate by hand. Ask tidy() to do it, and ask for a confidence interval at the same time.

broom::tidy(mod, exponentiate = TRUE, conf.int = TRUE)
term        estimate std.error statistic p.value conf.low conf.high
(Intercept) 0.000147  0.319       -27.6   <2e-16  7.79e-5  2.73e-4
age         1.124     0.00476      24.6   <2e-16  1.114    1.135
With exponentiate = TRUE, the estimate column becomes an odds ratio. The conf.low and conf.high columns give its 95% confidence interval.

The intercept row is also exponentiated, but it is rarely reported. Focus on the predictor rows.

Another study runs the same kind of model for smoking and reports an odds ratio of 1.25. The odds in the smoking group are how many percent higher? Type just the number.

Above 1, the percent higher is the OR minus 1. Here 1.25 minus 1 = 0.25, so 25 percent.

How this looks in a paper

Papers report the odds ratio, its confidence interval, and the p value in one sentence. For our model:

"Each additional year of age was associated with 12% higher odds of weak grip (OR 1.12, 95% CI 1.11–1.13, p < 0.001)."
OR 1.12: the exponentiated coefficient, 1.124 rounded.
12% higher odds: the OR in words. 1.12 is 12% above 1.
95% CI 1.11–1.13: the conf.low and conf.high columns.
p < 0.001: the p.value column.

In the sentence above, where does the 12% come from?

The percent wording is just the OR restated. OR minus 1 = 0.12, so 12% higher odds.

Comparing groups

Predictors are often groups instead of numbers. In the lessons, age was cut into three groups: 45–64, 65–74, and 75+. R uses the first group, 45–64, as the reference. Every odds ratio compares one group with that reference.

mod3 <- glm(weak_grip ~ age_group, family = binomial, data = klosa)
broom::tidy(mod3, exponentiate = TRUE, conf.int = TRUE)
term           estimate std.error statistic p.value conf.low conf.high
(Intercept)      0.0958   0.0693     -33.8   <2e-16   0.0834    0.110
age_group65-74   5.22     0.0936      17.7   <2e-16   4.35      6.28
age_group75+    14.4      0.119       22.5   <2e-16  11.5      18.3

In a paper this becomes:

"Compared with men aged 45–64, men aged 65–74 had 5.22 times the odds of weak grip (95% CI 4.35–6.28), while men aged 75+ had 14.44 times the odds (95% CI 11.44–18.22)."

The odds ratio of 5.22 compares men aged 65–74 with which group?

R compares every group with the reference, the first level. Here the reference is 45–64.

One last paper sentence: "Regular exercise was associated with lower odds of weak grip (OR 0.70, 95% CI 0.55–0.89)." What does this mean?

The OR is 0.70, so the odds are 1 minus 0.70 = 30% lower. The OR says nothing about grip strength in kilograms, only about the odds of weak grip.

Summary

Probability: a share of the whole group. It is between 0 and 1.
Odds: times it happens divided by times it does not (counts or probabilities). It equals 1 at a 50-50 chance.
Log-odds: the log of the odds. It can be any number. Logistic regression fits a straight line on this scale.
Odds ratio: the odds in one group divided by the odds in another. Exponentiating a coefficient gives one. In R: tidy(mod, exponentiate = TRUE, conf.int = TRUE).

You can now follow the whole chain: the model fits log-odds, exponentiating gives odds ratios, and papers report odds ratios with confidence intervals. That is exactly how you will report your own models in R.
