Probability, odds, log-odds, and odds ratios

You have already fit logistic regression models and read coefficients on the log-odds scale. This practical fills in the number scales behind those coefficients, then shows how to get and report odds ratios in R.

While doing logistic regression, we often move between four related ways to describe how likely an event is:

Probability, then odds, then log-odds, and finally odds ratios when we report results. They all describe the same underlying chance, but on different number scales.

These three measures, probability, odds, and log-odds, describe the same chance in different ways.

Probability, odds, and log-odds are three scales for the same underlying chance.

Probability is a share of the group

Imagine 10 people. An event happened to some of them, for example they passed a test. The coloured dots are the ones it happened to.

Probability asks: out of everyone, what share did it happen to? Here it happened to 4 of 10 people.

$p = \dfrac{4}{10} = 0.40$

Probability is always between 0 (never) and 1 (always).

Here is another group of 10 people. What is the probability that the event happened to a person?

Count the coloured dots, then divide by 10. Write it as a decimal, for example 0.5.

Odds compare the two sides

Probability compares the event to the whole group. Odds compare the two sides directly: how often it happens (or happened), against how often it does not.

Here are 10 people. The event happened to 8 and did not happen to 2.

$\text{odds} = \dfrac{\text{times it happens}}{\text{times it does not}} = \dfrac{8}{2} = 4$

Odds of 4 means the event happens 4 times as often as it does not. You can also say it is 4 times as likely to happen as not. Same idea, past count or future chance.

The odds of an event are 4. Which sentence is correct?

Odds = times it happens ÷ times it does not. The answer 4 is above 1, so the "happens" side is the larger one. That can mean a past count or a probability.

Odds below 1

Now take another group of 10 people. The event happened to only 2 and did not happen to 8.

$\text{odds} = \dfrac{2}{8} = 0.25$

The odds are below 1. The event is less common, or less likely, than not.

To flip a small odds value, use 1 ÷ odds. Here 1 ÷ 0.25 = 4. So odds of 0.25 means the event is 4 times less common (or less likely) than not.

The odds of an event are 0.25. Is the event more or less common (or likely) than not?

Odds below 1 mean the "happens" side is smaller than the "does not" side, in counts or in probability.

Odds of 1

When the two sides are equal, the odds are exactly 1. That is a 50-50 chance.

Odds above 1: the event is more common, or more likely, than not.
Odds equal to 1: a 50-50 chance.
Odds below 1: the event is less common, or less likely, than not.

The event happened to 5 people and did not happen to 5. What are the odds?

Odds = times it happens ÷ times it does not = 5 ÷ 5.

The event happened to 6 people and did not happen to 3. What are the odds?

Odds = times it happens ÷ times it does not = 6 ÷ 3.

Tap the group where the event is more common (or more likely) than not (odds above 1).

Odds above 1 means the "happened" (teal) stack is taller.

Probability and odds

Probability and odds describe the same chance. With probabilities, odds is how likely the event is, divided by how likely it is not:

$\text{odds} = \dfrac{p}{\,1-p\,}$

Odds are not balanced

Start from a 50-50 chance (odds = 1). Watch the odds line.

Making the event much more common sends the odds up to 9, then 90, with no upper limit.

Making it much less common can only push the odds down to about 0.11, then 0.01, but never below 0. The "less common" side is squeezed between 0 and 1.

One side has no limit, the other side stops at 0. The scale is not balanced. That makes odds hard to model with a straight line.

Which sentence is true about the odds scale?

Odds are a ratio of two positive sides (counts or probabilities), so they can never be negative. But they can grow as large as you like.

What is a log?

A log counts how many times you multiply to reach a number. Start at 1. On this ruler, each step to the right multiplies by 10. The log counts the steps.

1 is 0 steps, so its log is 0. Reach 10 in one step, log 1. Reach 100 in two steps, log 2. Go one step back to 0.1, log minus 1.

Log-odds uses a log that multiplies by about 2.7 each step instead of 10. That step size is a fixed number called e. It is close to 2.718, a bit like the number pi (about 3.14): just a constant that shows up often in maths. You never have to work it out by hand here. The computer does the log for you.

The rules stay the same, whatever the step size: the log of 1 is 0, above 1 is positive, below 1 is negative.

The sign of a log

A number above 1 has a positive log.
The number 1 has a log of 0.
A number below 1 has a negative log.

The region below 1, where the odds were squeezed, is now spread out on the negative side.

A number above 1 has a log that is:

Numbers above 1 grew from 1 by multiplying. That gives a positive log.

What is the log of 1?

At 1 you have not multiplied at all yet. Zero steps taken.

Is the log of 0.3 (a number below 1) positive or negative?

Below 1 means you had to divide (shrink) to get there. That counts as steps backward.

Log-odds

Take the odds, then take the log. That is the log-odds, also called the logit.

$\text{log-odds} = \ln(\text{odds}) = \ln\!\left(\dfrac{p}{1-p}\right)$
Very unlikely: log-odds is a large negative number.
A 50-50 chance: log-odds is 0.
Very likely: log-odds is a large positive number.

We show seven probabilities, from 0.05 to 0.95. Probabilities crowd near 0 and 1 on the left, but their log-odds spread out evenly on the right.

The point p = 0.50 has log-odds 0, in the middle. Above it is positive. Below it is negative.

On the diagram, which probability has log-odds equal to 0?

Log-odds 0 is the middle of the right line. That matches a 50-50 probability.

Read the rung at p = 0.90. The odds there are 0.90 ÷ 0.10. What is that value?

0.90 ÷ 0.10 is the same as 9 ÷ 1.

At p = 0.25 (below the middle), is the log-odds positive or negative?

p = 0.25 is below 0.50. Below 0.50 the odds are below 1, and the log of a number below 1 is negative.

Connecting to your logistic regression model

You have already fit a logistic regression for weak grip from age on the KLOSA data. The model summary gave you coefficients on the log-odds scale:

$\text{log-odds of weak grip} = -8.82 + 0.117 \times \text{age}$

Each extra year of age adds 0.117 to the log-odds. That fixed step is why the log-odds scale is useful for regression.

In papers you rarely report log-odds directly. You report odds ratios instead. The rest of this practical shows how to get them in R and how to read them.

In the model above, the age coefficient 0.117 is on which scale?

glm(..., family = binomial) fits a straight line on log-odds. Probabilities come later, through the logistic function.

From a log-odds difference to an odds ratio

A one-unit increase in age adds 0.117 to the log-odds. That is a difference on the log-odds scale.

To move to odds, undo the log with exp() (exponentiate). The result is an odds ratio (OR): how many times larger (or smaller) the odds are for each extra year of age.

$\text{OR for age} = e^{0.117} \approx 1.12$
OR = 1: no change in odds.
OR above 1: higher odds as the predictor increases.
OR below 1: lower odds as the predictor increases.

An odds ratio of 1.12 for age means:

An OR is a multiplicative change in odds, not an additive change in probability. (1.12 − 1) × 100% ≈ 12% higher odds.

Odds ratios you might see in a paper

Authors often translate an OR into plain language. Here are common patterns:

0.8020% lower odds
0.5050% lower odds, or half the odds

A results sentence says: "Heavier mothers had half the odds of a low birth weight baby." Which odds ratio matches?

Half the odds means the odds are multiplied by 0.5, so OR = 0.50.

When OR is well above 2, authors usually say "X times the odds" rather than a large percent.

Getting odds ratios in R with broom

After glm(..., family = binomial), the default summary() table shows log-odds coefficients. Use broom::tidy() with exponentiate = TRUE to get odds ratios instead.

library(broom) grip_model <- glm(weak_grip ~ age, family = binomial, data = klosa) tidy(grip_model, exponentiate = TRUE, conf.int = TRUE)

Setting conf.int = TRUE adds a 95% confidence interval for each OR, ready for a table.

Categorical predictors: reference group

With age groups instead of continuous age, R picks a reference level (the first category). Other rows are ORs compared to that reference.

klosa$age_group <- cut(klosa$age, breaks = c(45, 65, 75, Inf), labels = c("45-64", "65-74", "75+"), right = FALSE) group_model <- glm(weak_grip ~ age_group, family = binomial, data = klosa) tidy(group_model, exponentiate = TRUE, conf.int = TRUE)
# age_group65-74 estimate ≈ 5.22 # age_group75+ estimate ≈ 14.4

Men aged 65-74 had about 5.22 times the odds of weak grip compared with men aged 45-64 (the reference).

How this appears in a paper

A typical results sentence combines the OR, the comparison, and the 95% CI:

"Each additional year of age was associated with 12% higher odds of weak grip (OR 1.12, 95% CI 1.11 to 1.13; p < 0.001)."

For age groups:

"Compared with men aged 45-64, men aged 65-74 had 5.22 times the odds of weak grip (95% CI …), and men aged 75+ had 14.4 times the odds (95% CI …)."

Read this sentence from a hypothetical paper: "Each additional year of age was associated with 12% higher odds of weak grip (OR 1.12, 95% CI 1.11 to 1.13)." What does the OR tell you?

OR changes odds, not probability directly. Probability still follows the S-shaped logistic curve.

Summary

Probability: a share between 0 and 1.
Odds: happens ÷ does-not. Equals 1 at 50-50.
Log-odds: ln(odds). Logistic regression fits a straight line here.
Odds ratio: exp(log-odds coefficient). This is what you report in papers.
In R: tidy(model, exponentiate = TRUE, conf.int = TRUE) from broom.
