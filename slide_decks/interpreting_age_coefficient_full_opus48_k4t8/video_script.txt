Interpreting the Age Coefficient
===

Welcome back. We have fit a logistic regression for the probability of a weak grip, and the
summary gave one number for age, zero point one one six eight. In this lesson we answer three
things. What does that number mean, how do we turn it into something we can report, and how do
we write it in a paper.

--- PANEL 1 : the model and the number -----------------------------------------

Here is the model we fit. Each dot is one man in the data, at one if he had a weak grip and
zero if not. The gold curve is the fitted model, the probability of a weak grip by age. Notice
that the change in probability per year is not the same everywhere. It is small at young ages,
larger in the middle, and small again at old ages. So there is no single number for the change
in probability. And yet the summary gives us just one number for age, zero point one one six
eight. That number is not a change in probability. It is a change in the log-odds. Let us see
what that means.

--- PANEL 2 : the same model on two scales -------------------------------------

We start from the probability form and rearrange it, step by step, until we reach the log-odds
form. We find one minus p, then we divide p by one minus p, and after taking the natural log of
both sides we get: the log of p over one minus p equals beta zero plus beta one times age. Now
watch the plot on the right. When I press the arrow, the y-axis changes from probability to
log-odds, and the curve becomes a straight line. It is the same model on a different scale. A
probability must stay between zero and one, but a log-odds can run from minus infinity to plus
infinity, which is exactly the room a straight line needs. And on this straight line the age
effect is simply the slope. Each extra year adds zero point one one six eight to the log-odds.
That is our coefficient.

--- PANEL 3 : from coefficient to an odds ratio --------------------------------

A change in log-odds is still hard to picture. So we exponentiate it. Adding on the log scale
becomes multiplying on the odds scale, so the coefficient becomes a multiplier on the odds. e
to the zero point one one six eight is one point one two four. In R you do not have to do this
by hand. The tidy function from broom, with exponentiate set to TRUE, gives you the odds ratio
and its confidence interval directly. For age it is one point one two four, with a confidence
interval from one point one one to one point one three. Now, how do we read this? An odds ratio
above one means higher odds, below one means lower odds. To say it as a percent, subtract one
and multiply by a hundred. So one point one two four is twelve percent higher odds per year.
The table on the right collects a few values. An odds ratio of two is twice the odds. Zero point
five is half the odds. And one important warning: an odds ratio is not a risk ratio, so say
higher odds, not more likely. In a paper, you report the odds ratio with its confidence
interval, for example: the odds of weak grip rose twelve percent per year of age, odds ratio
one point one two, ninety five percent confidence interval one point one one to one point one
three. Here is a quick practice
question. Age has an odds ratio of one point one two. Which is the correct way to write it?
Pause and think. The answer is B, twelve percent higher odds, because an odds ratio is about
the odds, not about how likely something is.

--- PANEL 4 : report it, with probabilities and a sentence ---------------------

The odds ratio is the effect, but readers also want a concrete number they can picture. For that
we report a predicted probability. We simply put an age into the model. In R, we build a small
data frame of ages and call predict with type equals response. That type matters. Type equals
response gives a probability, not a log-odds. The model predicts about fourteen percent at age
sixty and sixty three percent at age eighty, shown as the gold dots. Putting it together, one way
to write the result is this. Older age was associated with higher odds of weak grip, with an odds
ratio of one point one two per year of age, and a confidence interval from one point one one to
one point one three. And the predicted probability rose from about fourteen percent at age sixty
to sixty three percent at age eighty. Report the odds ratio with its confidence interval, add a predicted probability
or two, and never report the raw log-odds. One last practice question. What does predict, with
type equals response, return? Pause and think. The answer is B, a probability, not a log-odds.
See you in the next lesson.
