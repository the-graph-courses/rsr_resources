KLoSA Wave 1 (2006) · 4,184 Korean men aged 45+ · low grip = grip ≤ 27 kg

From a straight line to a probability curve

1 Begin with measured grip strength

Each dot is one person. A straight line describes the average relationship between age and grip strength.

$\widehat{\text{grip}} = 55.68 - 0.382(\text{age})$   →   the same −0.382 kg per year everywhere
2 Change the scientific question

Perhaps exact kilograms are not the target. Ask instead: at this age, what is the probability of low grip? Recode each person as 1 = low, 0 = not low.

The 0/1 scatter contains the outcome we need, but the pattern is hard to see.
3 Make the probability pattern visible

Group nearby ages and calculate the percentage with low grip. The mean of a 0/1 variable is the proportion equal to 1.

Ages 65–69: $169/647 = 0.26$   →   estimated probability = 26%
4 Why not fit another straight line?
Boundaries: a line can predict below 0 or above 1. Those are impossible probabilities.
Shape: a line forces the same probability-point change everywhere. Many risks instead rise slowly, faster through the middle, then slowly near 1.
5 Wrap a straight line inside a logistic curve
$p = \dfrac{1}{1 + e^{-(\beta_0+\beta_1\,\text{age})}}$

This is a logistic curve, one member of the S-shaped sigmoid family. Whatever goes in, the result stays between 0 and 1.

glm(weak_grip ~ age, family = binomial)
$\widehat{\beta}_0=-8.824$   $\widehat{\beta}_1=0.1168$

The software fits the individual 0s and 1s. The grouped bars only help us see the pattern. In Desmos, use $1/(1+e^{-(a+bx)})$: $a$ shifts the midpoint; $b$ controls direction and steepness.

For now

The logistic function bends the hidden line into a valid probability curve. Unlike linear regression, one extra year does not produce a constant change in probability.

Same people · new scientific question

What if we care about a low-grip cutoff instead?

A different outcome
Maybe we do not need exact kilograms. Clinicians define a cutoff for low grip strength (here, ≤ 27 kg for men).
New question

At a particular age, what is the probability that a person falls into the low-grip category?

R
klosa <- klosa |>
  mutate(weak_grip = as.integer(grip <= 27))
#> 1 = low grip
#> 0 = not low grip
That turns each person into a zero or a one.
The 0/1 scatter is messy
Many points sit on 0, many sit on 1. The overall pattern is hard to see.
So summarize by age group
For each age band, compute the percentage with low grip. The mean of a 0/1 variable is that proportion.
Read one bar

Ages 65–69: 169 / 647 = 26%. That is an estimated probability.

Tempting next step

Why not fit a straight line through these probabilities?

Two problems
1 · Boundaries

Probabilities must stay between 0 and 1. A straight line has no such restriction. Extend it far enough and it predicts impossible values.

2 · Shape

A straight line assumes a constant change in probability. But when a probability is near 0 it often changes slowly, faster through the middle, then slowly again as it nears 1.

What we want instead

A curve that always stays in (0, 1), rises or falls smoothly, and flattens near the boundaries. That S-shape is a sigmoid.

The curve logistic regression uses

The logistic curve: put a straight line inside a sigmoid

Basic logistic function
$p = \dfrac{1}{1 + e^{-x}}$
Here \(e \approx 2.718\). The exact value matters less than what the formula does: whatever you put in for \(x\), \(p\) stays between 0 and 1.
\(x\) very negative

\(p\) near 0

\(x = 0\)

\(p = 0.5\)

\(x\) very positive

\(p\) near 1

Naming

This S-curve is the logistic function. (The logit is the inverse move we will uncover next: from probability back to the straight line.)

In Desmos: \(f(x)=1/(1+e^{-(a+bx)})\). Slider \(a\) shifts the midpoint; \(b\) controls steepness and direction.
Put a regression line inside
$P(\text{low grip}) = \dfrac{1}{1 + e^{-(\beta_0 + \beta_1\,\text{age})}}$
The inside, \(\beta_0 + \beta_1\,\text{age}\), looks exactly like ordinary linear regression. The logistic function wraps around that line and bends it into a probability curve.
R
glm(weak_grip ~ age, family = binomial, data = klosa)
#> (Intercept)  -8.82
#> age           0.117
Software chooses \(\beta_0\) and \(\beta_1\) so the observed 0s and 1s are reasonably likely under the model (binomial GLM with a logit link). Plug those numbers into the formula and you get this fitted curve.
Where the lesson pauses for now

The catch: probability does not change by a constant amount

Linear vs logistic reading
Linear regression

The coefficient is a constant change in the outcome. Here: about \(-0.38\) kg per year of age, everywhere.

Logistic regression

On the probability curve, the change is not constant. The curve is flatter near 0 and 1, steeper in the middle.

So what does change by a constant amount?
Uncover the straight line hidden inside the logistic. Let \(\eta = \beta_0 + \beta_1\,\text{age}\). Then:
$p = \dfrac{1}{1 + e^{-\eta}} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \log\!\left(\dfrac{p}{1-p}\right) = \eta$
$\log\!\left(\dfrac{p}{1-p}\right) = \beta_0 + \beta_1\,\text{age}$
The key idea

Left side = logit, also called log odds. Logistic regression is still linear: not in probability, but in log odds. So \(\beta_1\) is the constant change in log odds for a one-year increase in age.

Later we will turn that log-odds slope into something easier to say out loud. For this intro: S-curve for probability, straight line for log odds.
