Reporting Logistic Regression: Odds Ratios and Probabilities
===

Welcome back. In the last lesson we saw that the age coefficient, when we exponentiate it,
becomes an odds ratio of one point one two four. Now we get to the practical part. You have
run your model. How do you get this number out of R, how do you read it, and how do you write
it in a paper? That is what this lesson is about.

--- PANEL 1 : get the odds ratio out of R --------------------------------------

Remember that the summary output is on the log-odds scale. Those raw numbers are not what we
report. We turn them into odds ratios by exponentiating. The easiest way is the tidy function
from the broom package. You pass your model and you set exponentiate equals TRUE and conf dot
int equals TRUE. Now the estimate column is the odds ratio, and conf dot low and conf dot high
are the confidence interval. For age, the odds ratio is one point one two four, with a ninety
five percent confidence interval from one point one one four to one point one three five. If
you prefer to do it by hand, you can wrap coef and confint inside exp and get the same thing.
The idea is simple: start on the log-odds scale, and exponentiate to get the odds ratio.

--- PANEL 2 : reading an odds ratio --------------------------------------------

Now, how do we read this number? An odds ratio of exactly one means no effect. Above one means
higher odds. Below one means lower odds. To say it as a percent, there is a simple rule. If the
odds ratio is above one, subtract one and multiply by a hundred. So one point one two four is a
twelve percent increase in the odds for each year of age. If the odds ratio is below one, do one
minus the odds ratio times a hundred. Two anchors are worth remembering. An odds ratio of two is
twice the odds, which is a hundred percent higher. An odds ratio of zero point five is half the
odds, which is fifty percent lower. The small table on the right collects these. And one
important warning. An odds ratio is not a risk ratio. So please do not say twelve percent more
likely, or twice as likely. Say twelve percent higher odds. This is a very common mistake.

--- PANEL 3 : predicted probabilities ------------------------------------------

The odds ratio is the effect. But readers also want a concrete number they can picture. For
that we report a predicted probability. We simply put an age into the model. In R, I build a
small data frame with the ages I care about, fifty, sixty, seventy, and eighty. Then I call
predict on the model with type equals response. That type is important. Type equals response
gives a probability, not a log-odds. The model predicts about five percent at fifty, fourteen
percent at sixty, thirty four percent at seventy, and sixty three percent at eighty. You can
see those points as the gold dots on the fitted curve. If you also want confidence intervals on
these predictions, the predictions function in the marginaleffects package is a good tool.

--- PANEL 4 : write it in a paper ----------------------------------------------

Finally, let us put it into a sentence. Here is one way to write the result. Among four thousand
one hundred and eighty-four Korean men aged forty-five and older, older age was associated with
higher odds of weak grip. Each additional year raised the odds by twelve percent, with an odds
ratio of one point one two and a confidence interval from one point one one to one point one
three. And to make the size of the effect concrete, the predicted probability of weak grip rose
from about fourteen percent at age sixty to sixty three percent at age eighty.

A short checklist. Report the odds ratio and its confidence interval, and a p-value. Say it in
plain words, like twelve percent higher odds, and not twelve percent more likely. Do not report
the raw log-odds coefficient as your result. And add a few predicted probabilities so that your
reader can picture how large the effect really is.

That is how we report a logistic regression. See you in the next lesson.
