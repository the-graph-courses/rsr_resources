The Model

The S-Curve: Turning Numbers into Probabilities

Logistic regression uses a sigmoid function to map any input to a probability between 0 and 1. Think of it as a dial that starts near 0, rises through a tipping point, and levels off near 1.

The blue curve is the fitted model. The blue dots on the curve are the model's predicted probabilities. The red and green dots are where the actual outcomes sit (0 or 1).
Deconstructing the Formula

What Each Piece Actually Does

The logistic regression formula has layers. Let's peel them apart visually.

LAYER 1: The linear prediction

β₀ + β₁ × x = linear predictor
β₀ = baseline. Where do you start when hours = 0?
β₁ = slope. How much does each hour shift you?
x = input. Hours studied.

LAYER 2: Squash it through the sigmoid

p = 1 / (1 + exp( − linear predictor ))

The sigmoid is just a squashing machine. Feed in any number from −∞ to +∞, and it spits out a number between 0 and 1. Negative inputs produce values below 0.5. Positive inputs produce values above 0.5.

CONCRETE EXAMPLE: Student D (4 hours)

−3.572 + 1.429 × 4 = 2.144
p = 1 / (1 + exp(−2.144)) = 0.895 → ~0.788

A positive linear predictor means the probability lands above 0.5.

Analogy

Fitting = Tuning a Radio Dial

Imagine two knobs on a radio. One knob is β₀ (shifts the curve left/right) and the other is β₁ (makes the curve steeper/flatter). "Fitting" means twisting both knobs until you find the position where the curve best matches the data.

But how do we define "best"? We need a scoring rule that measures how well a particular curve matches the observed outcomes. That scoring rule is the likelihood.

R# R tunes the knobs for us model <- glm(pass ~ hours, family = binomial, data = df) coef(model) # (Intercept) hours # -3.57178 1.42871
Key Concept

The Confidence Scoring Game

Here is the intuition behind likelihood. Before each result is revealed, the model announces how confident it is. Then we score it:

If the student PASSED

Score = predicted probability

"You said 79%? Your score is 0.79."

If the student FAILED

Score = 1 − predicted probability

"You said 27% chance of passing? That means 73% chance of failing. Score: 0.73."

The overall score is the product of all individual scores. A perfect forecaster who always predicted 100% for pass and 0% for fail would score 1.0. A terrible one approaches 0.

Likelihood = score₁ × score₂ × score₃ × score₄
Why multiply? Think of it like AND logic. The model needs to get student A right and student B right and student C right... If any single prediction is terrible (near 0), it drags the whole product down.

Key Concept

Why Take the Log?

Products of small numbers shrink fast. With 100 students instead of 4, you would be multiplying 100 numbers between 0 and 1. That product quickly becomes astronomically tiny (like 10−47). Computers can not handle that.

The log transform is purely practical. It does not change which model is best. Maximizing the likelihood is the same as maximizing the log-likelihood. It just makes the math tractable.
RlogLik(model) # 'log Lik.' -1.909548 (df=2)
Key Concept

Maximum Likelihood Estimation (MLE)

We can now state what "fitting" means precisely:

MLE finds the β₀ and β₁ that produce the highest possible total score (likelihood)

Equivalently: the least negative log-likelihood

Think of it as an exhaustive search through all possible S-curves, scoring each one, and picking the winner. R does this iteratively using an algorithm called IRLS.

The Big Idea

Deviance = A Penalty Score for Bad Fit

Likelihood tells us "how good is this model?" Deviance flips that into "how bad is this model?" Think of it as a golf score: lower is better, and zero means perfection.

Deviance = −2 × log-likelihood
R# Computing deviance from log-likelihood -2 * as.numeric(logLik(model)) # [1] 3.819096 # R stores it directly model$deviance # [1] 3.819096
Deconstructing −2

Why Multiply by Negative Two?

Each part of the "−2" does a specific job:

−

The negative sign

Log-likelihoods are always ≤ 0 (because we are logging numbers between 0 and 1). Negating flips the scale so bigger = worse fit.

2

The factor of 2

This is a mathematical convenience. When you take the difference of two deviances, it follows a chi-squared distribution, which lets us compute p-values.

An analogy: temperature can be measured in Celsius or Fahrenheit. The underlying heat is the same; the scale just makes certain comparisons easier. The "−2" rescales the log-likelihood onto a scale where statistical theory gives us clean hypothesis tests.
A Subtlety

The Perfect Forecaster (Saturated Model)

Technically, deviance is measured as a gap between your model and a saturated model that fits every single data point perfectly.

🏆 Saturated Model

One free parameter per observation. Predicts each outcome with 100% confidence.

Likelihood = 1.0
Log-likelihood = 0

📊 Our Model

Two parameters (β₀, β₁). Imperfect predictions.

Likelihood = 0.276
Log-likelihood = −1.910

Deviance = −2 × (ℓour model − ℓsaturated)
= −2 × (−1.910 − 0) = 3.82
For binary outcomes, this simplifies. When every observation is 0 or 1, the saturated model's log-likelihood is exactly 0. So deviance just equals −2 × log-likelihood. That is why you will see both formulas in textbooks, and they are equivalent here.

The Key Question

The Deviance Drop: Is It Real?

Adding hours reduced deviance from 5.545 to 3.819. That is a drop of 1.726. But here is the catch:

Adding any predictor will almost always reduce deviance at least a little, even a totally random one. The question is whether the drop is large enough that it is unlikely to have happened by chance.

Likelihood Ratio Test

Comparing Against What Chance Would Produce

The Likelihood Ratio Test asks: if hours truly had no effect, how often would we see a deviance drop of 1.726 or more, purely by luck?

Under the null hypothesis (no real effect), the deviance drop follows a chi-squared distribution with degrees of freedom = number of parameters added (here, 1).

Our value (1.726) falls well to the left of the 3.84 cutoff. That means a drop this large could easily happen by chance.

Rdiff_dev <- null_model$deviance - model$deviance pchisq(diff_dev, df = 1, lower.tail = FALSE) # [1] 0.1889
Interpreting the Result

What Does p = 0.189 Mean Here?

If hours truly had zero relationship with passing, there is about a 19% chance we would see a deviance improvement at least this large, just from random noise in 4 data points.

That is not rare enough to be convincing (we typically need < 5%). So we say:

We fail to reject the null hypothesis. We cannot conclude that hours significantly improves model fit. The Lazy Forecaster might be doing just as well as the Smart one.

But wait, the pattern is clearly 0, 0, 1, 1. So what happened?

Low power. With only 4 observations, even a real effect is hard to detect. The LRT needs a sufficient sample size to distinguish signal from noise. With 40 students showing the same pattern, the p-value would be tiny.
R# Same pattern but with 10× more data hours_big <- rep(c(1,2,3,4), each = 10) pass_big <- rep(c(0,0,1,1), each = 10) big_model <- glm(pass_big ~ hours_big, family = binomial) anova(big_model, test = "Chisq") # Deviance drop: 17.26, p = 0.0000325
Practical R Usage

The One-Line Shortcut

You never have to compute any of this manually. R's anova() runs the entire Likelihood Ratio Test:

Ranova(null_model, model, test = "LRT")
R outputAnalysis of Deviance Table Model 1: pass ~ 1 Model 2: pass ~ hours Resid. Df Resid. Dev Df Deviance Pr(>Chi) 1 3 5.5452 2 2 3.8191 1 1.7261 0.1889

Every column maps to a concept we have already covered:

Resid. Dev = deviance of each model
Deviance = the drop (Δ)
Df = extra parameters added
Pr(>Chi) = p-value from χ² test
Shortcut with one model: anova(model, test = "Chisq") tests each predictor sequentially against the null. With a single predictor, this gives the same result.

Presenter Script

Welcome. This deck is about a specific, practical question: when you fit a logistic regression model, how do you know if it actually fits the data well?

The tool we will learn is called deviance. But deviance is built on top of maximum likelihood estimation, and the test we use to interpret it is the likelihood ratio test. So we will build up from first principles.

Everything uses just 4 data points. That is intentional. With 4 points, nothing is hidden and you can trace every number by hand.

Here is the data. Four students studied for an exam. Students A and B studied less and failed. Students C and D studied more and passed.

The pattern is clean: more hours, more likely to pass. But with only 4 observations, can we actually be confident that hours matters? That is the question deviance will help us answer.

Think of this as a prediction contest between two forecasters.

The Lazy Forecaster does not even look at hours. Since 2 out of 4 students passed overall, the lazy forecaster just says "50-50" for everyone. This corresponds to the null model, which only has an intercept.

The Smart Forecaster uses hours. Students with more hours get a higher predicted probability. This is our logistic regression model.

Our goal: determine whether the smart forecaster is meaningfully better than the lazy one, or whether the improvement could just be noise.

Here is the logistic regression curve fitted to our data. The S-shape is the key feature. It starts near 0 for low hours and rises toward 1 for high hours.

The blue dots on the curve are the model's predicted probabilities. For Student A with 1 hour, the model predicts only a 10.5% chance of passing. For Student D with 4 hours, it predicts 78.8%.

Notice how the actual outcomes (the red and green dots at 0 and 1) are separate from the predictions (the blue dots on the curve). The gap between them is what we are measuring.

Let's break the formula into layers, because this is where most people's eyes glaze over.

Layer 1 is just a straight line: intercept plus slope times hours. That is familiar from ordinary regression. It can spit out any number, including negatives.

Layer 2 wraps that straight line inside a sigmoid function, which squashes it to a number between 0 and 1. That is how we get a probability.

The concrete example at the bottom makes it tangible. For Student D with 4 hours, the linear predictor is about 2.1, and the sigmoid converts that to roughly 79%.

I like the radio tuning analogy here. You have two knobs: the intercept shifts the entire curve left or right, and the slope makes it steeper or flatter.

"Fitting" the model means twisting both knobs until the curve passes as close to the data as possible. The dashed curves show positions that do not match the data as well.

But "close to the data" is vague. We need a precise scoring system, and that is what the likelihood function provides.

This is the core idea. For every student, the model puts a stake in the ground: a predicted probability of passing. Then we score it based on what actually happened.

If the student passed, your score equals your predicted probability. So predicting 80% and being right earns you 0.80. If the student failed, your score is 1 minus your prediction. Predicting 10% chance of passing, and they indeed fail, earns you 0.90, because you were 90% confident in the correct outcome.

The total likelihood is the product of all four scores. Multiplying makes sense because you need to get every student right, not just one of them.

Here is the scorecard in action. Walk through each row.

Student A: the model said only 10.5% chance of passing. The student failed. So the model was 89.5% confident in the right answer. Score: 0.895. Great.

Student C is the weak spot. The model said 53.6% chance of passing, and the student passed. Score: 0.536. That is barely better than a coin flip. The model was not very confident here.

Multiply all four scores together: 0.895 times 0.731 times 0.536 times 0.788 equals 0.276. That is the total likelihood of the data under this model.

This slide explains why we take the logarithm. The visual tells the story: when you multiply numbers less than 1, the product shrinks rapidly. With 100 students, the product would be something like 10 to the negative 47th power. Computers round that to zero.

Taking the log converts multiplication into addition. Now instead of multiplying 0.895 times 0.731, we add negative 0.111 plus negative 0.313. The numbers stay in a manageable range no matter how many students we have.

The important thing is that the log does not change which model wins. It is a monotonic transformation. Whatever maximizes the likelihood also maximizes the log-likelihood.

Now we can state MLE precisely. Maximum Likelihood Estimation finds the values of beta-0 and beta-1 that produce the highest log-likelihood, or equivalently the least negative value.

R's glm function does this automatically. The output shows our fitted coefficients and, critically, those two deviance numbers at the bottom: 5.545 and 3.819. We are now ready to understand what those mean.

Deviance flips the log-likelihood into a penalty score. Instead of "higher is better," now "lower is better." Zero would mean a perfect fit.

The formula is simple: multiply the log-likelihood by negative 2. For our model, that is negative 2 times negative 1.91, which equals 3.82.

The visual scale shows where our model sits. Closer to zero is better. The null model is further out at 5.55. Our model is closer to zero at 3.82. The gap between them, 1.73, represents the improvement from using hours as a predictor.

A common question: why negative 2, specifically? Each piece does a job.

The negative sign flips the direction. Log-likelihoods are always zero or negative (because you are logging probabilities, which are between 0 and 1). Negating makes bigger numbers mean worse fit.

The factor of 2 is a mathematical convenience. It arranges things so that when you take the difference of two deviances, that difference follows a chi-squared distribution. Without the 2, you would need to adjust the chi-squared tables.

Here is a subtlety that often confuses people. Deviance is technically the gap between your model and a saturated model. The saturated model has one parameter per observation and fits the data perfectly.

For binary data, this simplifies beautifully. A perfect prediction means predicting 1.0 for every student who passed and 0.0 for every student who failed. The likelihood of that is 1.0, and log of 1 is 0. So the saturated model's log-likelihood is exactly zero.

That is why the deviance formula collapses to just negative 2 times the log-likelihood. This is specific to binary outcomes. For other types of GLMs, the saturated model's log-likelihood is not zero, and the full formula matters.

Now we put the two forecasters side by side. The lazy forecaster predicts 50% for everyone. The smart forecaster gives tailored predictions.

Look at the probability bars. The lazy forecaster's bars are all the same length. The smart forecaster's bars vary: short for students who failed, long for students who passed. That differentiation is exactly what a good model should do.

The deviance scores quantify this: 5.545 for the null versus 3.819 for the full model. Lower is better, so the full model wins. But by how much?

The deviance drop is 1.726. But here is the critical insight: even a random, meaningless predictor will usually reduce deviance at least a little bit. Adding any variable gives the model more flexibility, and more flexibility means a better fit to the specific data you have, even if the variable is noise.

So the question is not "did deviance drop?" It is "did it drop by more than we would expect from chance alone?"

This is the Likelihood Ratio Test. We compare our observed deviance drop of 1.726 against a chi-squared distribution with 1 degree of freedom.

The visual shows where our value falls on the chi-squared curve. The critical cutoff for alpha equals 0.05 is 3.84. Our value of 1.726 is well to the left of that cutoff, deep inside the "could happen by chance" region.

The p-value is 0.189. That means: if hours truly had no effect, there is about a 19% chance you would see a deviance improvement at least this large just by luck. That is too common to be convincing.

So what do we conclude? With p equals 0.189, we cannot reject the null hypothesis. The smart forecaster was not significantly better than the lazy one.

This might feel counterintuitive since the data is perfectly separated: 0, 0, 1, 1. But four data points is simply not enough evidence. The LRT needs sufficient sample size to distinguish a real pattern from noise.

The R code at the bottom shows what happens with 10 times as many students in the same pattern. The deviance drop scales up to 17.26, and the p-value drops to 0.00003. Same pattern, same effect, but now we have the power to detect it.

In practice, you use R's anova function to do everything in one call. The output table gives you both models' residual deviance, the deviance drop, the degrees of freedom, and the p-value.

Each column maps directly to a concept we have covered. The presenter can point to each column header and recall the corresponding analogy or formula.

Here is the full chain from start to finish. We fit two models, score each one with the likelihood, convert to deviance, measure the drop, and test it against a chi-squared distribution.

Every step has a clear job, and every step maps to a one-line R function. The entire workflow is three lines of code: fit the null, fit the full model, and call anova.

With a larger dataset, this exact same logic applies. The formulas do not change. The only difference is that you have more statistical power, making it easier to detect real effects.
