The t-test family | each type, its hypotheses, and its direction

One-sample

One group's mean vs a fixed target.

e.g. is a batch's mean dose really the labelled 500 mg?

Gold line: 500 mg target. Red bar: batch mean.
R
pill <- c(498.2, 501.5, 499.7, 502.3, 500.8,
          497.6, 503.1, 499.0, 501.9, 500.4,
          498.8, 502.7)
t.test(pill, mu = 500)        # alpha = 0.05
#> t = 0.94, df = 11, p-value = 0.368
#> p > 0.05  ->  fail to reject H0
Hypotheses & direction

$H_0:\ \mu = 500$

  • two-sided$H_1:\ \mu \ne 500$t.test(pill, mu = 500, alternative = "two.sided")
  • one-sided$H_1:\ \mu > 500$t.test(pill, mu = 500, alternative = "greater")
  • one-sided$H_1:\ \mu < 500$t.test(pill, mu = 500, alternative = "less")

Here: two-sided. Over- or under-dosing both fail, so watch both tails. The cautious default.

A nurse compares the resting heart rate of 20 athletes with the adult norm of 72 bpm, looking for any difference. Which alternative hypothesis fits?C

  • A. $\mu > 72$
  • B. $\mu < 72$
  • C. $\mu \ne 72$

For the athlete heart-rate test against the adult norm of 72 bpm, what is the null hypothesis?B

  • A. $\mu > 72$
  • B. $\mu = 72$
  • C. $\mu < 72$

Her test returns $p = 0.02$ at $\alpha = 0.05$. What should she conclude?A

  • A. Reject H0
  • B. Fail to reject H0
  • C. H0 is proven
Two-sample (independent)

Means of two separate groups.

e.g. does painkiller A or B give more relief?

Two independent clouds. Teal bars: group means.
R
A <- c(4.1, 5.2, 3.8, 4.9, 4.4,
       5.0, 3.5, 4.7, 4.2, 5.3)
B <- c(6.3, 5.8, 6.9, 6.1, 7.2,
       5.5, 6.6, 7.0, 6.2, 5.9)
t.test(B, A)                  # alpha = 0.05
#> t = 7.04, df = 17.9, p-value < 0.001
#> p < 0.05  ->  reject H0
Hypotheses & direction

$H_0:\ \mu_A = \mu_B$

  • two-sided$H_1:\ \mu_A \ne \mu_B$t.test(B, A, alternative = "two.sided")
  • one-sided$H_1:\ \mu_B > \mu_A$t.test(B, A, alternative = "greater")
  • one-sided$H_1:\ \mu_B < \mu_A$t.test(B, A, alternative = "less")

Either can apply. Use one-sided only if "is B better?" is the whole question, decided before the data.

Researchers compare mean cholesterol in two separate groups: one on a Mediterranean diet, one on their usual diet. What is the null hypothesis?A

  • A. $\mu_\text{med} = \mu_\text{usual}$
  • B. $\mu_\text{med} < \mu_\text{usual}$
  • C. $\mu_\text{med} \ne \mu_\text{usual}$

Why are two-sided t-tests often preferred in clinical drug testing?B

  • A. They make it easier to claim a drug works by lowering the p-value.
  • B. They detect either improvement or underperformance, and reduce directional bias.
Paired (dependent)

The same group measured twice.

e.g. does BP fall from before to after a drug?

Each before→after pair becomes one difference, tested against 0.
R
before <- c(150,148,162,142,155,149,163,158)
after  <- c(141,148,151,137,161,140,152,150)
t.test(after, before, paired = TRUE) # a=0.05
t.test(after - before, mu = 0)       # same
#> t = -2.76, df = 7, p-value = 0.028
#> p < 0.05  ->  reject H0
Hypotheses & direction  ($d=$ after $-$ before)

$H_0:\ \mu_d = 0$

  • two-sided$H_1:\ \mu_d \ne 0$t.test(after, before, paired = TRUE, alternative = "two.sided")
  • one-sided$H_1:\ \mu_d < 0$t.test(after, before, paired = TRUE, alternative = "less")
  • one-sided$H_1:\ \mu_d > 0$t.test(after, before, paired = TRUE, alternative = "greater")

For each study, name the t-test and a fitting $H_1$:

1. Same runners timed before and after an 8-week program, hoping for faster times.paired, $H_1:\ \mu_d < 0$

2. Mean recovery time for two surgical methods, on two separate groups of patients.two-sample, $H_1:\ \mu_1 \ne \mu_2$

3. Mean volume in a sample of vials, checked against the labelled 0.5 mL.one-sample, $H_1:\ \mu \ne 0.5$
