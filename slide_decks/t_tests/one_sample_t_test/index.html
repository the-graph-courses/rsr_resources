hmmmm... is this enough evidence that Bob is cheating?
Two possibilities:
1. Recipe really is 10g, 11.39g is just natural variation.
2. Recipe really is above 10g.
Which one? Let's settle it with a formal hypothesis test.
Hypothesis test framework
State a null hypothesis (H0) and an alternative hypothesis (H1).
H0 is the status quo. The true mean sugar is 10g. $H_0:\ \mu = 10$
H1 is what we want evidence for. Two flavors:
One-sided (we use first)

$H_1:\ \mu > 10$
true mean sugar exceeds the claim.

Two-sided (the default)

$H_1:\ \mu \ne 10$
true mean sugar differs from the claim in either direction.

Measuring the gap: Is 1.39g a lot?
By itself, 1.39g is unclear. We need another scale, one that accounts for how much the sugar content naturally varies among donuts, and how many donuts we weighed.
Variance: how scattered?
Sample size: how much data?

For the same average gap, the evidence for a significant difference is stronger when the data are less ____ (variable / consistent) and the sample size is ____ (larger / smaller).variable · larger

Step 1: standard deviation, $s$
$s$ measures how much individual donuts vary. Average distance from the mean.
$s = \sqrt{\dfrac{\sum_i (x_i - \bar{x})^2}{n - 1}} = 2.59\text{ g}$
Step 2: standard error, $\mathrm{SE}$
Means wobble less than single donuts. Divide $s$ by $\sqrt{n}$.
$\mathrm{SE} = \dfrac{s}{\sqrt{n}} = \dfrac{2.59}{\sqrt{10}} = \color{#b5451f}{0.82\text{ g}}$
Step 3: $t$ statistic
Standardised gap: how many SEs above the claim?
$t = \dfrac{\bar{x} - \mu_0}{\mathrm{SE}} = \dfrac{1.39}{0.82} = \color{#b5451f}{1.70}$
R
s  <- sd(sugar)
se <- s / sqrt(length(sugar))
t  <- (mean(sugar) - 10) / se
#> t = 1.70
Step 4: $p$-value
Compare $t$ to a $t$ curve with $df = n - 1 = 9$. One-sided $H_1:\ \mu > 10$, so $p$ = the upper tail only.
$t = 1.70$, upper-tail $p = 0.062$.
R
pt(t, df = 9,
   lower.tail = FALSE)
#> 0.062
