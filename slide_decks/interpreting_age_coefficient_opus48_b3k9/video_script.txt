Interpreting the Age Coefficient: Log-Odds and Odds Ratios
===

[DRAFT narration. It follows the three panels. Rewrite freely in your own voice while
recording. The wording is kept simple and direct, with no em dashes, because many
viewers are learning in English. Numbers use the same rounding as the R summary output
(0.1168), so viewers can match them by eye. Timings are placeholders.]

[00:00:00]

Welcome back. In the last video we fit our logistic regression. We stopped at an open
question. Here again is the figure we finished with. Each dot is one man in the data. A dot
at the top, at one, means the person had a weak grip. A dot at the bottom, at zero, means
they did not. The gold curve is the model we fit. And in the summary output there was one
number for age, zero point one one six eight. We said this number explains the whole
relationship, but we did not say what it means. That is what we will do in this video.

--- PANEL 1 : the model, and the number 0.1168 --------------------------------

First, look at the curve. The change in probability for each extra year is not the same
everywhere. At young ages the curve is almost flat, so one more year changes the probability
very little. In the middle the curve is steep, so one more year changes it a lot. At the top
it is flat again. So we cannot give a single number for the change in probability per year.
It is different at every age.

But the summary gives us just one number for age, zero point one one six eight. Here is the
model written in its probability form, first with the general symbols and then with our
numbers, minus eight point eight two four and zero point one one six eight. So one fixed
number is doing the work, even though the change in probability is not fixed. The reason is
that zero point one one six eight is not a change in probability. It is a change in the
log-odds. Let us see what a log-odds is, on the next panel.

--- PANEL 2 : the same model on two scales ------------------------------------

We start with the probability form. p equals one over one plus e to the minus eta, where eta
is beta-zero plus beta-one times age. Now we change it one step at a time.

First we find one minus p. We write one as the fraction one plus e-to-the-minus-eta over
itself, then subtract. This gives e-to-the-minus-eta over one plus e-to-the-minus-eta. Next
we divide p by one minus p. To divide by a fraction, we flip it and multiply. The parts
cancel, and we are left with one over e-to-the-minus-eta, which is e-to-the-eta. Finally we
take the natural log of both sides. The log of p over one minus p equals eta, which is
beta-zero plus beta-one times age.

Now I want you to see that these two forms are the same model. On the right is the
probability curve, inside the box from zero to one. Watch the plot when I press the right
arrow. The y-axis changes from probability to log-odds, and as it does, the curve becomes a
straight line. It is the same data and the same model. Only the vertical scale changed.

Look at what each dashed line did. Each line is one probability moving to its log-odds value.
To convert, first find the odds, which is p over one minus p, then take the natural log. A
probability of one half gives odds of one, and the log of one is zero, so it sits in the
middle. A probability of zero point seven five gives odds of three and a log-odds of about
plus one point one. A probability of zero point nine five gives odds of nineteen and a
log-odds of about plus two point nine. Values above one half are positive. Values below one
half are negative. And notice the box is gone. A probability must stay between zero and one.
A log-odds can be any value from minus infinity to plus infinity. That extra room is why a
straight line can fit on the log-odds scale.

--- PANEL 3 : beta-one is a log odds ratio ------------------------------------

Now we can say what beta-one means. From panel two, the log-odds is a straight line in age.
Let us write it at age a, and then again one year later at age a plus one. At age a plus one
we get beta-zero plus beta-one times a, plus one more beta-one. Now subtract the first line
from the second. Everything cancels except beta-one. The left side is the log-odds at a plus
one minus the log-odds at a. A difference of logs is the log of a ratio, so this is the log
of the ratio of the two odds. So the log of the odds ratio equals beta-one.

To get the odds ratio by itself, we undo the log by raising e to each side. e to the zero
point one one six eight is one point one two four. This is the number we report. It means
each extra year of age multiplies the odds of a weak grip by one point one two four, which
is a twelve point four percent increase in the odds. The multiplier is the same at every
age. You can see this on the bars, from age seventy to age eighty. Each single year
multiplies the odds by the same one point one two four. Ten of these steps together give
about three point two, so the odds are about three times larger over ten years.

This is why we kept the same rounding. The zero point one one six eight in the log-odds
column of the summary and the one point one two four odds ratio are the same fact on two
scales. In R, exp of coef of the model gives one point one two four, and exp of confint of
the model gives the confidence interval.

--- PANEL 4 : which form to use, and why --------------------------------------

Let us step back. Regression has two main jobs, and the two forms of the equation match
them.

The first job is prediction. If you want to predict a probability, you use the probability
form, the logistic function. You put in an age and you read out a probability. But on the
probability scale there is no single effect, because the change per year depends on the age.

The second job is understanding the effect of a variable. For that we use the log-odds form,
the straight line. On this scale the effect is a single number, beta-one, which is zero
point one one six eight added to the log-odds for each year.

The last problem is that a change in log-odds is hard to picture. So we exponentiate it.
There is nothing tricky here. Yes, e to the power of a log removes the log. The one point to
notice is that the change in log-odds is a difference of two logs, and when you exponentiate
a difference you get a ratio, because e to the A minus B equals e to the A divided by e to
the B. So exponentiating the change in log-odds gives the ratio of the two odds, which is the
odds ratio, one point one two four. It is a multiplier, not an added amount.

So we have one coefficient understood in three ways. It adds on the log-odds scale, it
multiplies on the odds scale, and on the probability scale the amount of change depends on
age. In the next videos we will use odds ratios with more than one predictor. See you there.
