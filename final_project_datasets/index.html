Some important notes about the data catalog

Expect to find problems in the data. We have looked through all of these datasets and done a lot of work to curate a set of data that are straightforward to analyse, but you may still run into issues. For example, the data shared by the authors may not exactly match what the paper reports, or there may be miscodings or typos.

Plan your work time on this project with that expectation in mind. You can report such issues and inconsistencies as part of your analysis.

Your goal is not to replicate the analyses in the papers. Many of the source papers used methods quite different from what we learned in class.

Many of the papers also used complex sampling designs (clustered or weighted surveys). We have not covered multilevel models or advanced survey data analysis, so it is fine to analyse these with the simple regression methods from the course. You may choose to report this as a limitation in your paper.

Not all predictors work for all the outcomes. Think about whether it makes sense conceptually. Here are a few ways it could fail to make sense:

  • The predictor comes after the outcome. For example, in the Peru dataset, birth_weight was determined at birth, so a child's current haemoglobin_g_dl cannot be a predictor of it.
  • The predictor is a composite part of the outcome. For example, in the Brazilian medical student dataset, dreem_learning is one of the subscales that make up dreem_global.
  • You have both a continuous variable and the categorised version of the same thing. For example, do not use systolic_bp to predict hypertension, because they are the same measurement.

Missing data. Because you are not expected to impute missing values, rows with missing values will be dropped when you run your regressions. Keep this in mind when using variables that have a lot of missing values, especially in multivariable regressions, where the losses add up across predictors.
